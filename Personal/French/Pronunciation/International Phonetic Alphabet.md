The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) is a system designed so that **one symbol always equals one sound**. Spelling in many languages is notoriously chaotic—in English, "read" rhymes with either "seed" or "bed" depending on the tense. The IPA solves this by providing a universal, exact map of pronunciation.

Because the full global IPA contains over 100 letters, this guide focuses on the core sounds you'll encounter in English, plus a few essential additions you'll need when learning European languages.

## 1. Consonants

Most IPA consonants look exactly like the standard alphabet and make the exact sound you expect:

- **/p/, /b/, /t/, /d/, /k/, /g/, /m/, /n/, /f/, /v/, /s/, /z/, /h/, /l/, /w/** (e.g., /p/ in **p**at, /m/ in **m**ap).
    

The ones you need to memorize are the unique symbols:

|**Symbol**|**Sound**|**Example Word**|
|---|---|---|
|**/ʃ/**|"sh"|**sh**ip, na**ti**on|
|**/ʒ/**|"zh"|mea**s**ure, vi**si**on|
|**/tʃ/**|"ch"|**ch**ur**ch**, ma**tch**|
|**/dʒ/**|"j"|**j**udge, ma**g**ic|
|**/θ/**|soft "th"|**th**ink, ba**th**|
|**/ð/**|hard "th"|**th**is, mo**th**er|
|**/ŋ/**|"ng"|si**ng**, ba**n**k|
|**/j/**|"y"|**y**es, **y**ellow|
|**/ɹ/ or /r/**|"r"|**r**ed, ca**r**|

## 2. Vowels (Monophthongs)

Vowels in the IPA are trickier because we make many more vowel sounds with our mouths than the five standard letters we write with.

**Short Vowels:**

|**Symbol**|**Sound**|**Example Word**|
|---|---|---|
|**/ɪ/**|short i|b**i**t, s**y**stem|
|**/ɛ/ or /e/**|short e|b**e**d, s**ai**d|
|**/æ/**|short a|c**a**t, b**a**ck|
|**/ʌ/**|short u|c**u**p, l**o**ve|
|**/ʊ/**|short oo|b**oo**k, p**u**t|
|**/ɒ/ or /ɔ/**|short o|d**o**g, w**a**sh|
|**/ə/**|"uh"|**a**bout (the unstressed "Schwa" sound)|

**Long Vowels** (The colon `:` indicates the sound is stretched out):

|**Symbol**|**Sound**|**Example Word**|
|---|---|---|
|**/i:/**|long e|s**ee**, m**ea**n|
|**/ɑ:/**|long ah|f**a**ther, c**a**r|
|**/ɔ:/**|long aw|c**augh**t, b**oa**rd|
|**/u:/**|long oo|bl**ue**, f**oo**d|
|**/ɜ:/**|long er|b**ir**d, w**or**d|

## 3. Diphthongs (Gliding Vowels)

These are two vowel sounds that glide together to form a single syllable. Notice how the symbol literally shows the starting and ending mouth positions.

|**Symbol**|**Sound**|**Example Word**|
|---|---|---|
|**/aɪ/**|"eye"|m**y**, l**igh**t|
|**/eɪ/**|"ay"|d**ay**, m**a**ke|
|**/ɔɪ/**|"oy"|b**oy**, c**oi**n|
|**/aʊ/**|"ow"|c**ow**, l**ou**d|
|**/oʊ/ or /əʊ/**|"oh"|n**o**, b**oa**t|

## 4. Expanding to Other Languages (e.g., French)

When learning languages with different phonetic rules and accents, you will encounter new symbols for sounds that do not exist natively in English. A few distinct ones include:

- **/y/**: Pursing your lips to say "oo" but trying to make an "ee" sound (e.g., the _u_ in the French word _t**u**_).
    
- **/ʁ/**: A guttural "r" pronounced in the back of the throat (e.g., the _r_ in _Pa**r**is_).
    
- **/ɔ̃/, /ɑ̃/, /ɛ̃/**: The tilde (`~`) above a vowel means it is a **nasal vowel**, where air passes through the nose while speaking (e.g., _b**on**_, _b**lan**c_, _v**in**_).
